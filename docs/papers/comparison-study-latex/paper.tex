\begin{abstract}
We compare two model families for cross-corpus speech dysfluency detection:
self-supervised Wav2Vec~2.0 and compact mel-spectrogram CNNs. F1, the standard
metric here, depends on the decision threshold as well as the model,
and thresholds calibrated on one corpus need not transfer. Seven
configurations are compared: five independent binary Wav2Vec2 classifiers
(arm01); two shared-backbone multitask variants frozen for 3
and 20 epochs (arm02, arm03); and four compact mel-spectrogram CNNs with
different sequence aggregators (arm04--arm07). All are trained on 29{,}296
clips from SEP-28K and UCLASS, then evaluated under a
clip-level in-distribution split that shares speakers and a speaker-independent
cross-corpus split on 53 English Project Boli recordings. In-distribution, arm01 attains
the highest measured macro F1 (0.570, 95\% CI 0.554--0.587, validation-tuned
thresholds), with arm02 close behind (0.556, 0.540--0.572) at
${\approx}4.9\times$ fewer parameters; the intervals overlap. Cross-corpus, the
ordering inverts: an all-positive classifier attains macro F1 $0.550$
(0.490--0.604), which nothing exceeds, because the CNNs reach their F1
by labelling nearly every external clip positive (macro predicted-positive rate
0.879, 95\% CI 0.849--0.909, against a base rate of 0.408) while the Wav2Vec2
configurations collapse toward all-negative (0.079--0.102). Threshold-free
metrics reverse the ranking again: the task-adapted Wav2Vec2 configurations
rank external clips best (macro AUROC 0.620--0.621, AUPRC 0.546--0.557) and
the CNNs lie near chance. The predicted-positive rate shows the CNN operating
points already sat far above the in-distribution base rate, so the CNN advantage
under thresholded F1 is consistent with operating-point drift and does not
imply superior ranking. Thresholded F1 alone therefore cannot support
cross-corpus claims for this task. Each configuration was trained once;
intervals reflect clip-level resampling, and the external analysis is
exploratory.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Stuttering affects approximately 1\% of the global adult population and up to
5\% of children~\cite{yairi2009childhood}. It is characterized by involuntary
repetitions of sounds and words, abnormal prolongations, and pauses in speech
flow (blocks), and it affects the psychosocial well-being of those who stutter.
Clinical assessment by speech-language pathologists relies on subjective listening
and waveform inspection, which is time-consuming, subject to inter-rater
variability, and does not scale; this has motivated substantial work on automatic
stuttering identification~\cite{sheikh2022review}. Self-supervised speech
representation learning, and Wav2Vec~2.0 in particular~\cite{baevski2020wav2vec},
has since produced strong downstream results from raw audio. Applied to dysfluency
detection, however, this line of work is dominated by a single evaluation style:
models are trained and tested on a common corpus, and performance is summarized
with per-class and macro F1 at a threshold tuned on held-out data from that same
corpus. F1 at such a threshold measures two things at once: the quality of the
model's ranking of clips, and the suitability of a threshold calibrated on data
resembling the test data. Only the first should transfer to new speakers and
corpora. The two can move in opposite directions under shift: a threshold that
maximizes validation F1 in-distribution may fall in a degenerate region of the ROC
curve once the score distribution changes, leaving F1 dominated by class base
rates rather than discrimination. The external corpora available for this task are
small enough that a single operating point can dominate the reported result.

Cross-corpus and speaker-independent evaluation is not new in principle. Bayerl
et al.~\cite{bayerl2023crosscorpus} studied cross-corpus stuttering detection as
a multi-label problem, and Bayerl et al.~\cite{bayerl2022partitioning} showed that
the choice of dataset partitioning materially changes reported results. The
interaction between the two metric families under shift is less well
characterized: how much of a cross-corpus F1 difference is attributable to
ranking quality, and how much to operating-point drift. Without that separation,
comparisons between representation families risk being read as statements about
generalization when they concern threshold placement. We address this with a
controlled comparison of seven configurations, Wav2Vec~2.0 models and compact
spectrogram CNNs, under matched protocols, reporting thresholded and
threshold-free views side by side. Our research questions are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1}: Under conventional clip-level in-distribution
evaluation, how do the Wav2Vec2-based and compact spectrogram CNNs compare in
macro and per-class F1?
\item \textbf{RQ2}: On a speaker-independent external corpus, how do their
measured F1 scores and their ordering change?
\item \textbf{RQ3}: How much of the cross-corpus F1 behaviour is explained by
operating-point drift (a mismatch between predicted-positive rate and class
base rate) rather than by a change in ranking quality?
\item \textbf{RQ4}: Do threshold-free metrics (AUROC, AUPRC) give a
materially different account of cross-corpus behaviour than thresholded F1?
\end{itemize}

No configuration generalizes across corpora reliably
(Section~\ref{sec:results}). Our contributions
are: (i) a matched comparison of Wav2Vec~2.0 and compact spectrogram CNN
configurations under in-distribution and speaker-independent cross-corpus
protocols; (ii) an explicit separation of ranking quality from operating-point
placement, using the predicted-positive rate measured on both splits under
identical thresholds, which shows the two metric families yielding opposite
orderings on the external set; (iii) the observation that no configuration
exceeds an all-positive baseline on external F1, a negative result; and (iv) a
per-class analysis identifying interjection as
easiest and block as hardest.

% ── II. RELATED WORK ─────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Detection Approaches}
Early systems used handcrafted features (jitter, shimmer, spectral flux, formant
trajectories) with SVMs, HMMs, or GMMs~\cite{sheikh2022review} and generalized
poorly across speakers, accents, and conditions. End-to-end deep learning removed
the manual feature-engineering step: CNNs on mel-spectrograms with recurrent
layers showed early promise for multi-type dysfluency
detection~\cite{kourkounakis2020detecting}, as did RNNs and time-delay networks
for temporal dependencies~\cite{sheikh2021stutternet}. These remain attractive
where labelled data and compute are scarce, and define the compact end of the
capacity range compared against here. Self-supervised pre-training has since
become dominant: Wav2Vec~2.0 contrasts quantized latent speech units against
masked waveform spans and generalizes across languages when pre-trained
multilingually (XLSR-53~\cite{conneau2021xlsr}), HuBERT~\cite{hsu2021hubert}
predicts masked hidden units from offline clustering, XLS-R~\cite{babu2022xlsr}
scales the objective to 128 languages, and WavLM~\cite{chen2022wavlm} adds
denoising objectives. Bayerl et al.~\cite{bayerl2022multi} applied Wav2Vec2 to
multi-task stuttering detection on FluencyBank, reporting macro F1 of 0.56--0.63
across five types, and to UCLASS therapy sessions~\cite{bayerl2022therapy}; on
the standardized SEP-28k-E partition per-class F1 varies widely by type
(interjection 0.78, prolongation 0.53, block 0.30)~\cite{susac2026stuttmil}.
Common to these models is that their embeddings encode linguistic content together
with paralinguistic attributes such as speaker identity and recording condition;
how much corpus-specific information a representation carries is the trade-off
our comparison probes.

\subsection{Corpora and Metrics Under Shift}
The three corpora used here (Section~\ref{sec:arch}) differ in speakers,
recording conditions, label definitions, and annotation granularity,
so generalization to unseen conditions is difficult to assess. Language is not
among the differences that our evaluation actually exercises, because the
ingested Boli portion is English-only (Section~\ref{sec:arch}). Bayerl et
al.~\cite{bayerl2023crosscorpus} framed cross-corpus detection as a
multi-label problem, motivated by dysfluent events co-occurring rather than
appearing in isolation, and showed that the partitioning scheme changes reported
results substantially~\cite{bayerl2022partitioning}; most of this literature
still reports F1 at a threshold chosen on in-distribution data, the practice
whose adequacy we examine. Our study is methodological: we
hold the model set fixed, vary the evaluation protocol, and report thresholded
and threshold-free metrics for the same predictions.

F1 is the harmonic mean of precision and recall at a chosen operating point, so
its value depends on the threshold as well as on the score ranking. AUROC
summarizes ranking over all thresholds but can look optimistic when positives
are rare, because a large negative class inflates the area; AUPRC weights
precision by the positive class, is the more informative of the two under
imbalance, and has the positive-class prevalence rather than 0.5 as its chance
level. Predictive calibration is a separate property that we do not
measure~\cite{guo2017calibration}. We report, for each configuration and
protocol, thresholded F1, threshold-free AUROC and AUPRC, the AUPRC lift over the
prevalence prior, and the predicted-positive rate (PPR): at a fixed threshold, a
PPR far from the base rate indicates displaced operating-point placement rather
than lost discriminative ability. Because the same thresholds are applied to both
evaluation sets, the difference in PPR between them measures drift directly
rather than by inference.

% ── III. CORPORA AND MODELS ──────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Corpora and Models}
\label{sec:arch}

The system is a clip-level classification pipeline that predicts which of the
five dysfluency types are present in a 3-s audio window. All models share one
preprocessing module and are exposed through a common registry API.

\subsection{Source Corpora and Clip Construction}
SEP-28K contributes 32{,}321
clips (28{,}177 podcast clips and a 4{,}144-clip re-annotation of adult
FluencyBank speech); UCLASS contributes 4{,}712 therapy-session clips in
SEP-28K format; and Project Boli contributes 54 clips with word-level
annotations and timestamps; Boli is confined to the test split, so no external
speaker is seen in training. The three are merged into 37{,}087 manifest rows,
with per-clip interval CSVs recording events as
\texttt{(start\_sec, end\_sec, type)} tuples; clips whose audio is missing or
empty are dropped, leaving 36{,}674 usable clips. The 36{,}620 non-Boli clips
are partitioned 80/10/10 at random under seed 42 into 29{,}296 training, 3{,}662
validation, and 3{,}662 test clips, and all 54 Project Boli clips are then pinned
to the test split, giving a test partition of 3{,}716 rows. Audio is resampled
to 16~kHz mono, DC-removed, peak-normalized, and silence-trimmed.

Evaluation does not iterate over manifest rows. It enumerates the audio files
present in a split directory and keeps those that have a matching label CSV and
a non-empty body, so the number of scored clips is the number of distinct
scored recordings rather than the number of rows admitted. On the test partition
this yields 3{,}715 scored clips, of which 53 are Project Boli; the difference
between the 54 admitted rows and the 53 scored recordings is one row that this
filter does not admit. The same rule applied to the external evaluation set is
what produces the Boli count of 53 used throughout this paper.

\subsubsection{Construction of the Project Boli subset}
\label{sec:boli}
The external subset is derived by an explicit four-step selection procedure from
the released Boli corpus. First, all \texttt{.txt} files in the
\texttt{Transcripts} directory are
enumerated in sorted order; each is a word-level annotation list whose lines carry
a start time, an end time, and a dysfluency code. Second, each transcript is
paired with an audio file by name: a direct filename lookup is attempted, then a
mapped lookup through a fixed task table, then a recursive basename search; a
transcript with no resolvable audio is discarded. Third, the paired transcript is
parsed line by line, lines with fewer than three fields are skipped, and the
remaining codes (\emph{B}, \emph{PR}, \emph{SR}, \emph{WR}, \emph{IN}) are mapped
to block, prolongation, sound repetition, word repetition, and interjection; a
transcript yielding no recognized code is discarded. The 54 surviving transcripts
each contribute one manifest row whose five binary labels are set by whether any
annotated word carries the corresponding code. Fourth, all 54 rows are pinned to
the test split and never seen in training; scoring then enumerates distinct audio
filenames in that split, so the 54 rows resolve to 53 distinct scored recordings,
and all external results are computed on those 53 clips.

Because the pairing key is the audio filename, the scored unit is the distinct
recording rather than the annotation row, which is the mechanism behind the
54-to-53 reduction; the reports retain only the resulting counts, so we cannot say
which filter removed the single row. Three properties of the released corpus
matter for interpreting the external results. First, each annotated file is built
around a single stuttered word~\cite{boli2025}, so the external set is enriched
for dysfluent content by construction and is not a sample of naturalistic speech.
Second, the released corpus is multilingual: the Boli paper reports 28 participants who
stutter (22 Hindi, 2 Telugu, 2 Bengali, 1 Marathi, and 1 Assamese mother tongue),
contributing roughly 2.5~h of audio. Our ingestion path resolves only the English
task codes of that release, so all 54 clips we ingest are English and the
cross-corpus protocol below changes speakers, recording pipeline, and annotation
convention, but not language: it is a cross-corpus evaluation and not a
cross-lingual one, and cross-lingual robustness remains untested by these results.
Third, the retained audio segment is the first 3~s of
each file (Section~\ref{sec:win}), whereas the Boli label derives from the whole
file, so a recording longer than 3~s carries a label that need not be audible in
the segment the model receives.

\subsection{Annotation Harmonization}
The three corpora use different conventions, which we map onto five common
binary clip-level labels: prolongation, block, sound repetition, word
repetition, and interjection. SEP-28K labels are adopted as-is, with a clip
positive for a type when at least two annotators marked it
(\texttt{SEP28K\_MIN\_VOTES} $=2$); requiring two votes is a substantive choice,
since accepting any single vote inflates positives several-fold. Because each
UCLASS clip is a 3-s window centered on a stutter event, each present label is
materialized as a weak interval of half-width 0.25~s centered at 1.5~s, i.e.\
spanning 1.25--1.75~s. Project Boli provides
word-level tokens labeled \emph{B}, \emph{PR}, \emph{SR}, \emph{WR}, and
\emph{IN}, mapped through a fixed code table to the same five classes as
described in Section~\ref{sec:boli}, a clip being positive for a class when any
annotated word carries it. All models therefore consume identical
five-dimensional binary targets.

\subsection{Preprocessing and the 3-Second Window}
\label{sec:win}
SEP-28K clips are natively 3~s, so a 3-s maximum window matches the predominant
corpus; because the task is clip-level, an event whose acoustic extent crosses the
window boundary does not alter the target. Audio longer than 3~s is truncated to
its first 3~s and shorter audio is zero-padded, so every configuration receives an
identically shaped input. Two consequences recur below: silence trimming risks
suppressing the pause that defines a block, and for any Boli recording longer
than 3~s the label is derived from the whole file while the retained segment is
its first 3~s, so a positive label need not be present in the audio the model
receives (Section~\ref{sec:interpret}).

\subsection{Model Configurations}
Seven configurations are evaluated. Trainable parameter counts are 472{,}845{,}450
for arm01 (five independent Wav2Vec2 binary classifiers), 97{,}332{,}362 each for
arm02 and arm03 (Wav2Vec2 multitask, backbone frozen for 3 and 20 epochs), and
341{,}322, 274{,}242, 456{,}906, and 539{,}594 for arm04--arm07 (CNN with pooling,
single-head CNN with pooling, CNN with LSTM, and CNN with transformer). The CNNs
therefore hold 0.3--0.6\% of the parameters of arm01. The evaluation artifacts
report slightly larger figures for the CNNs---342{,}030, 274{,}950, 457{,}614, and
540{,}302---because they count every element of the stored checkpoint, including
708 BatchNorm buffer elements (running statistics) that are not trainable; we quote
the trainable counts here. Inference latency,
throughput, and peak GPU memory were not measured and are not inferred from these
counts (Section~\ref{sec:compute}).

\subsubsection{Model descriptions.}
arm01 handles each dysfluency type with a separate binary classifier on
\texttt{facebook/wav2vec2-base}, 94{,}569{,}090 parameters each: raw audio
$\rightarrow$ encoder $\rightarrow$ a learned projection of the frame-level hidden
states (\texttt{Linear}(768$\rightarrow$256)) $\rightarrow$ mean pooling over time
$\rightarrow$ a linear classifier (\texttt{Linear}(256$\rightarrow$2)) $\rightarrow$
softmax. Two details are easy to assume otherwise and are therefore worth stating:
the projection precedes the pooling rather than following it, and the head contains
no dropout layer. No attention mask is passed by the pipeline, so the temporal
reduction is an unmasked mean over frames; because every clip is zero-padded or
truncated to a fixed 3~s, all clips present the same number of frames. Each
classifier is trained independently with focal loss ($\gamma = 2.0$), AdamW
(lr $= 3\times10^{-5}$, weight decay $= 0.01$), a 500-step linear warmup, and
mixed precision, the backbone frozen for the first 3 epochs; once the backbone is
unfrozen it is trained with a reduced learning rate of $3\times10^{-6}$, one tenth
of the head rate. arm02 and arm03 instead feed five parallel per-class
heads from one shared encoder, each an MLP on a temporal mean pool
(\texttt{Linear}(768$\rightarrow$768) $\rightarrow$ Tanh $\rightarrow$
\texttt{Linear}(768$\rightarrow$2)), minimizing the summed focal loss, for 97.3M
parameters in total, only ${\approx}2.8$M more than one independent classifier,
because the backbone is shared. The two differ only in that arm03 freezes the
backbone for 20 epochs instead of 3, isolating freezing duration; the 3-epoch
freezing schedule and the post-unfreeze learning-rate reduction of arm01 apply to
both.

arm04--arm07 share a spectrogram backbone and differ in the sequence aggregator.
Input is a log-mel spectrogram with 128 Mel bands, a 2048-point FFT, and hop length
512. The encoder is four convolutional blocks with 32, 64, 128, and 128 channels,
each with a $3\times3$ kernel (padding 1), batch normalization, ReLU, a $(2,1)$
max-pool in all but the last block, and 2-D dropout at rate $0.4$. The three pools
halve the frequency axis three times and leave the temporal axis at 94 frames, so
the encoder output is a $128\times16\times94$ tensor (channels $\times$ mel bins
$\times$ frames). This is aggregated by global mean pooling plus a
\texttt{Linear}(128$\rightarrow$128) projection (arm04); a single-layer
unidirectional LSTM with hidden size 128 over the frequency-pooled time axis, using
the final hidden state (arm06); or a one-layer transformer encoder with model
dimension 128, four heads, feed-forward width 512, and dropout $0.4$, followed by
temporal mean pooling (arm07). Five per-class two-layer MLP heads
(\texttt{Linear}(128$\rightarrow$128) $\rightarrow$ Tanh $\rightarrow$
\texttt{Linear}(128$\rightarrow$2)) produce the logits. The CNN arms are trained
from scratch, so no backbone freezing or discriminative pre-training applies, and
their objective differs from that of the Wav2Vec2 arms: instead of focal loss they
use binary cross-entropy on the positive logit with a per-class positive weight set
to the negative-to-positive class ratio of the training pool, with the per-head
losses summed over the configured classes. That ratio is computed over the whole
training directory rather than over the training subset alone, a difference from
the multitask Wav2Vec2 trainer that we record but do not expect to matter. The two
families therefore differ in training objective as well as in representation, which
we note as a confound when comparing them. arm05 is the single-head variant of the
pool model: the head block is built once per configured class rather than five
times, so each arm05 model carries the same two-layer head but only a single
instance of it, and one such model is trained per dysfluency type (five models in
total), mirroring the five-binary Wav2Vec2 design at a much smaller scale.

% ── IV. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP ───────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Training Protocol}
All models are trained on a single NVIDIA GPU with seed $= 42$, 20 epochs and
early stopping (patience $= 5$), mixed precision, batch size 8 (binary
classifiers) or 16 (multitask and CNN), and 3~s audio at 16~kHz. Training reads
the training directory only: it derives its own held-out portion from it, a 20\%
stratified subsample (5{,}857 clips, seed 42) that serves as the early-stopping
and threshold-tuning set, so the parameters are fitted on 23{,}439 clips and the
prepared validation split is never opened. No gradient
clipping is applied; an earlier configuration that clipped the total gradient norm
was abandoned because, on a 94.6M-parameter model with a randomly initialized
head, the clip dominated the head's early gradient signal. The checkpoints
evaluated here were trained without input augmentation, so
we claim no augmentation effect. Each configuration is trained once, so every
reported value is a single-run point estimate.

\subsection{Threshold Selection}
Thresholds are tuned on a held-out 20\% stratified subsample of the training pool
(5{,}857 clips, seed 42) by sweeping 17 fixed values from
0.1 to 0.9 in steps of 0.05 and, per class, selecting the value maximizing
F1 on that subsample rather than Youden's J. That subsample is also the
early-stopping set, so the tuned thresholds are mildly optimistic; we treat this as
a limitation rather than as test-set leakage, since the test split is never used
for any selection. The prepared 3{,}662-clip validation split is not consumed by
the training or evaluation code and is unused in every number reported here.
arm02 uses thresholds of 0.55, 0.35, 0.40, 0.35, and 0.45 for prolongation, block,
sound repetition, word repetition, and interjection. No test-set or
cross-corpus threshold fitting is performed anywhere in this paper; the same thresholds are applied unchanged to the
external set, the transfer that Sections~\ref{sec:results}
and~\ref{sec:discussion} examine.

\subsection{Reported Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}
For each configuration, protocol, and class we report precision, recall, F1,
AUROC, and AUPRC, plus macro averages (the unweighted mean over the five classes),
which keep classes of differing prevalence from being dominated by the most
frequent; the behaviour of these metrics under shift is discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:related}. AUPRC is reported as a lift over its chance level, the
positive-class prevalence. Precision and recall are omitted from the main tables,
having fixed the threshold in advance, but are recoverable from F1 and the PPR
with the confusion counts.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocols}
\subsubsection{In-distribution, clip-level (Test).}
The test partition holds 3{,}716 manifest rows, of which 3{,}662 come from
SEP-28K and UCLASS and 54 from Project Boli. The partition operates on clips, not
speakers, so speakers may appear in both training and test for the SEP-28K and
UCLASS portions; these numbers are an upper-bound benchmark under
near-distribution conditions and are not speaker-independent. Results are
reported over the 3{,}715 scored clips, of which the 53 scored Boli recordings
(${\approx}1.4\%$) are out-of-domain. The macro base rate on this split is 0.143.

\subsubsection{Speaker-independent, cross-corpus (Boli).}
The 53 scored Boli recordings are unseen during training, with different speakers,
recording conditions, and label-construction procedure (Section~\ref{sec:arch}).
Per-class support is small: PR 16, BL 35, SR 34, WR 14, IN 9, giving a macro base
rate of 0.408. We treat all external conclusions as exploratory.

Two trivial baselines accompany the models: the \emph{all-positive} classifier,
which labels every clip positive for every class, and the \emph{random-at-rate}
classifier, which labels clips positive with probability equal to the class base
rate. All-positive is the more informative under heavy imbalance, because it
attains recall 1 by construction and its F1 is determined entirely by the base
rate, so a model that does not exceed it has not demonstrated usable
discrimination; the expected macro F1 of random-at-rate equals the macro base
rate.

\subsection{Statistical Uncertainty}
\label{sec:uncert}
Uncertainty is quantified by nonparametric bootstrap resampling of clips: for each
reported quantity we resample the evaluated clips with replacement 10{,}000 times,
recompute the metric, and take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. Resampling is
unstratified, so it propagates variability in class prevalence as well as in the
decisions; on the 53-clip external set the former dominates for the trivial
baselines. F1 at a fixed threshold and the predicted-positive rate are functions
of the confusion counts alone, so their bootstrap operates exactly on the stored
per-class confusion matrices. These intervals capture clip-level sampling
variability only: each configuration was trained once, so they exclude training
seed, and overlapping intervals should not be read as a real difference in model
quality. AUROC and AUPRC are reported as point estimates without intervals, because
both depend on the full per-clip score vector, which was not retained after
evaluation, so no bootstrap of them can be formed; we therefore treat the
threshold-free ordering as directional evidence and the external results as
exploratory.

The threshold-free view inverts the thresholded ordering once more. arm01 and
arm02 rank the external clips best, with macro AUROC 0.620--0.621 and macro AUPRC
0.546--0.557, a lift of ${\approx}1.3$--$1.4\times$ over the prevalence prior;
arm03, the over-frozen variant, sits near chance; and the CNNs are at or barely
above chance, with macro AUROC 0.473--0.522 and macro AUPRC 0.412--0.445. The
advantage is modest: these are point estimates without
intervals, and on 53 clips with at most 35 positives per class and 9 in the worst
case, a macro AUROC of 0.62 against 0.52 is well within the range that resampling
could plausibly reduce to nothing. We therefore state the result directionally:
the threshold-free metrics place the task-adapted Wav2Vec2
configurations above the CNNs, and no configuration comes near reliable
cross-corpus detection.

The same table quantifies the mechanism behind that disagreement, which is our
answer to RQ3. Because the two PPR columns apply identical thresholds to the two
evaluation sets, the comparison is a measurement rather than an inference. Three
facts follow.

First, the operating points are far from the prevailing base rate in both
protocols: for six of the seven configurations the 95\% interval for external
macro PPR excludes the external base rate of 0.408, the sole exception being
arm07 (0.381--0.494). The three Wav2Vec2 configurations sit far below the
external base rate, at 0.079--0.204, and the four CNNs above it, at 0.438--0.879.

Second, the CNN operating points were already displaced
before the shift. On the in-distribution split, where the base rate is 0.143, the
CNNs label 0.365--0.845 of clips positive, a factor of 2.6--5.9 above prevalence,
while reaching only 0.249--0.271 macro F1. The external F1 they appear to win is
thus not produced by the shift: the same over-positive operating point that
cripples their in-distribution F1 becomes advantageous externally only because the
Boli base rate (0.408) is nearly three times the in-distribution one. In relative
terms their operating point is \emph{less} displaced externally than
in-distribution (for arm06, 5.9 times the base rate on the test split against 2.2
times on Boli), the opposite of the drift a generalization failure would produce.

Third, the task-adapted Wav2Vec2 configurations move the other way under the same
thresholds, from 0.134 and 0.159 in-distribution to 0.079 and 0.102 externally,
toward all-negative. Their low external F1 is the mirror image of the CNNs'
behaviour; neither is a statement about ranking quality, because AUROC and AUPRC
do not move with the threshold and so order the two families the other way
round.

\subsection{Computational Cost and Relation to Published Results}
\label{sec:compute}
Parameter count, storage size, computational cost, and runtime cost are distinct:
the first two follow from the weights and a stated precision, the last must be
measured. We therefore report parameter counts and their fp32 storage in
Section~\ref{sec:arch} but not latency, throughput, or peak GPU memory, and we do
not infer them from parameter counts, which give fp32 storage of 1.89\,GB for
arm01 against 1.1--2.2\,MB for the CNNs. Within the Wav2Vec2 family we would
select the multitask configuration for operational reasons: 97.3M parameters
against 472.8M because the encoder is shared rather than replicated five times,
and one forward pass per clip instead of five. It does not measurably beat arm01. Our best in-distribution macro F1, 0.570 (five-binary)
and 0.556 (multitask), falls within the 0.56--0.63 reported for Wav2Vec2 on
FluencyBank~\cite{bayerl2022multi}, and our per-class ordering matches the 0.30
(block) to 0.78 (interjection) span on SEP-28k-E~\cite{susac2026stuttmil}; this is
an ordering comparison, not a numerical one, since our split shares speakers
between training and test and the corpora, label definitions, and threshold
strategies differ~\cite{bayerl2022partitioning}.

% ── VI. DISCUSSION ───────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the In-Distribution Results Show}
The Wav2Vec2 configurations capture in-distribution signal that the compact CNNs,
at roughly 0.3--0.6\% of the parameter count, do not. The most likely explanation
is representational capacity: a 94M-parameter pretrained encoder supplies features
that a four-block CNN trained from scratch on 29K clips cannot match. Capacity,
pretraining, and objective are not separable in our design, since the Wav2Vec2
configurations minimize focal loss while the CNNs minimize a class-balanced binary
cross-entropy and we vary the architecture family as a whole, so we do not
attribute the gap to any single factor. The one factor we can isolate, freezing
duration, acts in the opposite direction and is unambiguous: withholding task
adaptation from the same encoder costs ${\approx}32\%$ of tuned macro F1, an effect
larger than any difference among the CNNs.

\subsection{Why the External Results Are Hard to Interpret}
\label{sec:interpret}
Three features complicate every reading of Tables~\ref{tab:boli_perclass}
and~\ref{tab:boli_drift}: 53 scored recordings, per-class support as low as 9, and
a label-construction procedure unlike that of the training corpora. The bootstrap
intervals quantify the first two, and the third is the most consequential. The
wide intervals are not a formality: the external macro F1 of the strongest model,
0.508 (0.440--0.566), overlaps that of the all-positive baseline, 0.550
(0.490--0.604), so the negative result of Section~\ref{sec:res-boli-tuned} is a
statement about point estimates on a set too small to separate them. The one
comparison the data does support cleanly is the operating-point one: the
predicted-positive rate intervals in Table~\ref{tab:boli_drift} exclude the
prevailing base rate for six of seven configurations, and the same thresholds
applied in-distribution show the CNN operating points were already displaced
before the shift. That measurement, not the F1 ranking, is the substantive content
of the external evaluation.

Because Boli files are built around an annotated stutter
event~\cite{boli2025}, a positive Boli label reflects a curated event rather
than the outcome of sampling continuous speech and applying a majority vote,
which is how SEP-28K and UCLASS labels arise. The all-positive baseline
consequently scores 0.550 here against 0.247 on the test split, and that gap is at
least partly a property of how the external clips were selected rather than of any
model. A second complication is the label--window mismatch flagged in
Section~\ref{sec:arch}, which depresses recall for reasons unrelated to
representation family; we cannot determine how often it occurs, because we did
not retain the durations of the evaluated recordings.

\subsection{Proposed Explanations}
\label{sec:hypotheses}
Two explanations the present experiments do not distinguish are stated as
proposals rather than findings.

\emph{Operating-point drift.} The score distributions of all seven
configurations are displaced under the shift, so thresholds fixed
in-distribution land in different regions of the ROC curve for the two families,
and F1 then tracks the predicted-positive rate and the base rate rather than
discrimination. The direct evidence is the paired PPR columns of
Table~\ref{tab:boli_drift} and Figure~\ref{fig:charts}(a), in which the families
sit on opposite sides of the base rate, together with the ordering flipping when
the threshold is removed. Because both PPR columns use the same thresholds, the
displacement is measured rather than assumed. What the measurement does not
settle is why it occurs, since that would require the score distributions
themselves rather than their induced decisions; the disagreement between F1 and
AUROC/AUPRC is the expected signature of such displacement, which is exactly when
a single thresholded number invites misinterpretation.

\emph{Corpus-specificity.} The higher-capacity Wav2Vec2 representations may
encode corpus-specific characteristics of SEP-28K and UCLASS (speakers, accents,
recording conditions, annotation conventions) that transfer weakly to a corpus
built by a different procedure, while the coarse spectral patterns retained by a
mel-spectrogram CNN transfer more evenly. The threshold-free ordering is
consistent with this account, since arm01 and arm02 lead on AUROC and AUPRC while
the CNNs sit near chance, but it is equally consistent with the label--window
mismatch and label provenance above, and no measurement we have separates these.
A probe classifier on frozen embeddings predicting source corpus would be the
most direct test; we have not run one.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limitations}
\paragraph{Statistical power and single-run training.} This is the most
consequential threat, with two independent sources. Every configuration was
trained once from seed 42, so the arm01/arm02 difference of under 0.02 macro F1 is
not interpretable even though their bootstrap intervals overlap, and the arm03
degradation carries no training uncertainty either: the intervals in
Tables~\ref{tab:macro}--\ref{tab:perclass} describe clip sampling only. Separately,
the external set has 53 scored recordings with per-class support from 9 to 35,
which is why external F1 comparisons are point estimates and the threshold-free
metrics have no intervals. We use ``better'' and ``outperforms'' only where a
difference exceeds plausible seed and sampling variation.

\paragraph{Uncontrolled properties of the external set, and untested
explanations.} As detailed in Section~\ref{sec:arch}, the external labels are
built differently from the training labels, the ingested clips are English-only
and so the external set does not test language shift, and truncation to the
first 3~s may decouple labels from retained
audio. The window, trimming, and truncation policy is held fixed across all
configurations and both protocols, so it does not confound the comparisons
reported here, but it is an uncontrolled absolute factor and a plausible
contributor to the block-class difficulty. All tuned F1 values depend on
validation-F1-optimal thresholds, and we do not measure calibration, so we cannot
distinguish a miscalibrated probability head from a mismatched operating
point~\cite{guo2017calibration}; the two accounts in Section~\ref{sec:hypotheses}
remain proposals. Finally, capacity, pretraining, and objective are not separated,
latency and throughput were not measured, and a single external corpus cannot
distinguish a general failure of cross-corpus transfer from a property of this
corpus.

\section{Practical Implications and Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Model selection for this task cannot rest on in-distribution F1, because that
split shares speakers with training and the cross-corpus F1 ordering is dominated
by where a fixed threshold lands. Reported results should therefore include, at
minimum, a speaker-disjoint or cross-corpus split, threshold-free metrics
alongside F1, a trivial all-positive baseline, and the predicted-positive rate at
the reported threshold; each would have changed the conclusion we drew from the F1
columns alone.

On RQ1, Wav2Vec2 dominates in-distribution at macro F1 0.55--0.57 against
0.25--0.27 for the CNNs and 0.247 for an all-positive baseline, with the
five-binary and multitask variants indistinguishable and excessive freezing
costing ${\approx}32\%$ of tuned macro F1. On RQ2 the cross-corpus F1 ordering
inverts, with the CNNs appearing to lead, and on RQ3 that inversion is consistent
with operating-point drift rather than generalization: at identical thresholds the
CNNs label 37--85\% and 44--88\% of in-distribution and external clips positive
against base rates of 0.143 and 0.408, while the Wav2Vec2 configurations fall to
8--10\% externally, and no configuration exceeds the all-positive baseline of
0.550. On RQ4 threshold-free metrics reverse the ordering again, placing the
task-adapted Wav2Vec2 configurations above the CNNs at macro AUROC 0.620--0.621
against 0.473--0.522. The conclusions we can draw are narrow: F1 alone cannot
support cross-corpus claims for this task, and interjection is easier to detect
than block under both protocols. We cannot claim cross-corpus deployment, because
no configuration exceeded a trivial baseline on the external set of 53 scored
recordings with per-class support as low as 9 and at least three confounding
properties (Section~\ref{sec:arch}). The cause of the score drift and the modest
threshold-free advantage of Wav2Vec2 remain unresolved
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).
